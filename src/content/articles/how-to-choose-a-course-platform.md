Open the pricing pages of five course platforms and you'll find much the same checklist on each one. Courses. Community. Live sessions. Payments. Email. Almost every box is ticked almost everywhere.

That sameness is the problem. The tables make the platforms look interchangeable, and they aren't. Each one is built around one activity, with the other features arranged around it. A community platform is built around conversation. A funnel builder is built around the sale. A marketplace is built around search. When the platform's center matches where learning happens in your course, the software stays out of your way. When it doesn't, you spend your time working around it.

**Short answer:** Pick the platform category first and the product second. Match the category to how you teach. Community-first platforms fit teaching that is mostly ongoing conversation. WordPress LMS plugins fit creators who want control and can maintain software. Funnel builders fit businesses where selling is harder than teaching. Marketplaces fit creators who don't have an audience yet. Course-first platforms fit structured lessons, often taught to a group that moves through together.

A disclosure before going further: I co-founded Ruzuku, which is one of the course-first platforms in this article. I'll be specific about where the other categories would serve you better. Where I link to comparisons, they're pages my team keeps on ruzuku.com, and the prices in them carry dates.

## Why Do Feature Lists Make the Choice Harder?

Feature lists make platforms look interchangeable when each one is built around a different activity. In 1989, Michael Moore described three kinds of interaction in distance education: learners with content, learners with the instructor, and learners with each other (Moore, 1989). Every course you build will use all three. Most courses lean on one of them, though, and so does most software.

A video library leans on learners working with content. A coaching program leans on learners working with you. A cohort that works through problems together leans on learners working with each other. Terry Anderson and Jon Dron later traced how teaching approaches and technology have shaped each other across the history of distance education (Anderson & Dron, 2011). The tools you have make some kinds of teaching easy and others awkward.

So run the logic in the right order. Decide how you teach. Then look for software built around that.

A feature list can't do this for you. "Community: yes" can mean a full social network on one platform and a comment box under each lesson on another. Both ticks are accurate. They describe very different products.

## What Questions Define Your Teaching Model?

Four questions do most of the work:

1. **Where does the learning happen?** In the lesson, in conversation, in a live session, or in practice students do on their own.
2. **Who finds your students?** You, or the platform.
3. **Do students move through together or alone?** A shared start date, or enroll-any-time.
4. **What's the hard part of your business?** Getting people to buy, getting them to finish, or keeping the software running.

Your answers point to a category. Here's how they map:

| If your teaching is mostly... | Look first at... | What you give up |
|---|---|---|
| Ongoing conversation among members | Community-first platforms (Circle, Mighty Networks, Skool) | Course tools vary, and discussion usually sits beside the lessons |
| Custom-built, on a site you control | WordPress LMS plugins (LearnDash) | You maintain the software, plugins and hosting |
| A product sold through a launch or funnel | Funnel builders (ClickFunnels, Kartra, Systeme.io) | Live classes and cohort management |
| Pre-recorded, for people you can't reach yet | Marketplaces (Udemy, Skillshare) | Price control and the student relationship |
| Structured lessons, often with a group | Course-first platforms (Ruzuku, Teachable, Thinkific) | You find your own students |

The sections below take each category in turn: what it's built around, when it fits, and what it costs you.

## When Does a Community-First Platform Fit?

A community-first platform fits when the conversation among members is what you're offering. Circle, Mighty Networks and Skool are built around your members talking to each other. The course is one room in a larger house. Circle organizes discussion into spaces, a bit like channels in Slack, and adds events and member networking. Mighty Networks puts weight on member profiles and offers native mobile apps. Skool adds points, levels and leaderboards.

Think of a professional network, a mastermind that runs for years, or a membership people renew for each other rather than for the next lesson. In Moore's terms, these platforms are built for learners interacting with each other, without that interaction being tied to a particular piece of content.

The trade-off appears when you want structured teaching inside the community. Course tools vary across the three. On Circle, check two things before you commit: whether its quizzes and assignments let you review and reply to each student's work, and how you'll release lessons to a group on set dates. Running a cohort there takes more manual organization. Mighty Networks does offer quizzes and scheduled cohort courses. Skool releases lessons a set number of days after each member joins, rather than on dates you set for a whole group.

The bigger question is where discussion lives. On a community platform, the main conversation happens in spaces or feeds organized by topic. On a course-first platform, it can be attached to the lesson itself, so a student finishes your lesson and responds to your prompt in the same place.

Across 1.3 million enrollments on Ruzuku, courses with lesson-level discussions complete at 50.9%, against 37.3% without. This is observational data, not an experiment. Creators who add discussion prompts may differ in other ways from creators who don't. In two niches in our data the pattern runs the other way. Therapy and counseling courses complete at 31.9% with lesson discussions and 54.5% without; health and medical courses at 65.7% and 73.5%. We don't know why. If you teach in those fields, don't add discussion prompts because of the platform-wide number. Add them if conversation is how your students learn, and watch your own completion. And it tells you nothing about completion on community platforms, because we can't see their data. What it does suggest is that a conversation tied to the lesson does different work from a conversation next to it. I've written more about why in [Community as Pedagogy](/learning-design/community-as-pedagogy/).

## When Does a WordPress LMS Plugin Fit?

A WordPress LMS plugin fits when you already run WordPress and someone on your team likes maintaining it. LearnDash turns a WordPress site into a course platform. You install it on your own hosting, next to your theme and your other plugins. That gives you more control than any hosted platform. You can customize almost everything, and the license is priced by number of sites, not number of students. LearnDash also has a more advanced quiz builder than Ruzuku's.

Control comes with a job attached. You're responsible for hosting, security and updates to WordPress, LearnDash, your theme and every plugin. Payments, email and live sessions each need another plugin, and so does community beyond basic comments. When a student can't log in, the support request comes to you. As of November 2026, Liquid Web sells LearnDash as a bundle from $350 a year for one site, and hosting is extra.

In teaching terms, a plugin is neutral. It can support almost any teaching model, as long as you build it.

That makes this category a good fit for two kinds of creator. The first already runs a WordPress site and has someone, maybe themselves, who likes maintaining it. The second needs something a hosted platform doesn't offer, such as a custom connection to systems they already run. If neither describes you, maintenance becomes a monthly cost that doesn't make your teaching any better.

Our [Ruzuku vs LearnDash comparison](https://www.ruzuku.com/compare/learndash) sets out what the license does and doesn't include, and what hosting and plugins add.

## When Does a Funnel Builder Fit?

A funnel builder fits when selling is harder than teaching. ClickFunnels, Kartra and Systeme.io start from the sale. You get a page builder, a checkout and email marketing in one login, with automation that reacts to what a buyer did. Courses come as a module inside that sales system.

If your revenue depends on a launch sequence, an evergreen funnel or affiliates, keeping all of it on one bill makes sense. Your course is what the buyer gets at the end of the funnel.

The teaching tools differ more than you'd expect. As of August 2026, Systeme.io had a free plan and paid plans from $17 a month. Its quizzes and certificates were on every plan, the free one included, and it could release lessons on a drip schedule. ClickFunnels had no quiz builder at any price.

None of the three runs an interactive live class or manages a cohort. Kartra's webinars are broadcast-style, ClickFunnels hands you off to a paid Zoom plan, and Systeme.io says its webinars are for pre-recorded content. The closest any of them comes is releasing content to everyone on the same date. A shared release date is a schedule. A cohort is a group of people who know they're going through the course together. If you teach cohorts, the software has to know the group exists.

The test: if any part of your teaching happens live, or depends on people moving through together, a funnel builder means paying for a second tool and connecting the two. That can work well. You can run the funnel for selling and a course platform for teaching. Just plan for both from the start.

Our [comparison of Ruzuku with ClickFunnels, Kartra and Systeme.io](https://www.ruzuku.com/compare/sales-funnel-platforms) covers quizzes, live sessions and course limits plan by plan.

## When Does a Course Marketplace Fit?

A course marketplace fits when you have expertise but no audience yet. Udemy and Skillshare solve a different problem: finding students. Learners are already there, searching. You don't need an email list, an ad budget or a website to make your first sale.

You pay for that in two ways. The first is money and price control. When Udemy's marketplace finds the buyer for a web sale, Udemy takes a 3% processing fee and then keeps 63% of the rest, so you keep about 36% of the price. When the buyer comes through your own coupon or referral link, you keep about 94%. Udemy also caps list prices at $199.99 and can discount your course in its own promotions. Those are Udemy's terms as of November 2026. Skillshare works differently again: it pays per minute watched.

The second cost is the relationship. Udemy keeps the student. You can't email your students directly, and each sale stands on its own. A student who loved your course has no easy path to your next one, or to a live program you run somewhere else.

There's a teaching cost as well. Marketplaces are built for pre-recorded courses. There are no live sessions, and discussion is limited to Q&A. If your teaching depends on students working with each other, the format can't carry it.

This category still fits a real situation. You have expertise, no audience, and you want to find out whether people will pay to learn your topic. A marketplace does the finding for you. Some creators use it that way, as a starting point, and keep their deeper programs on their own platform.

## When Does a Course-First Platform Fit?

A course-first platform fits when learning happens in the work students do on each lesson, often as a group. Ruzuku, Teachable and Thinkific are built around the course itself: a sequence of lessons, with tools for checking understanding along the way. This is the category I work in, so you should weigh this section with that in mind.

You teach something with steps. Students practice, submit work, get feedback and move on. You care whether they finish.

Within the category, the products lean in different directions, so check the details against your own answers to the four questions. Teachable offers native mobile apps for iOS and Android and a built-in affiliate program. Thinkific has a large app marketplace and offers SCORM on Plus, its custom-priced top plan. Ruzuku leans toward teaching with discussion and live sessions inside the course. A discussion can be attached to each lesson. Modules can open on dates you set, so a cohort stays together. Built-in video meetings hold up to 60 people on camera, with breakout rooms, and students don't need a Zoom account. For comparison, Circle's live rooms hold 15 people on its Professional plan (as of November 2026). Students submit assignments and you give feedback. Ruzuku Core is $99 a month or $997 a year, with zero transaction fees, and a free plan for up to 5 students lets you build and test a course first.

Each product also has gaps. Ruzuku has no native mobile app and no SCORM support. Certificates are only on the Pro plan, at $199 a month. There's no funnel builder, no affiliate program and no built-in email marketing; you connect your email tool through Zapier. If certificates are central to your program, or your students expect an app, those are real reasons to look elsewhere.

The trade-off for the whole category is the one marketplaces solve. A course-first platform doesn't find your students. You do.

## Can You Combine More Than One Category?

Yes. The categories aren't exclusive, and you can pair them. Two pairings make particular sense.

The first is a marketplace for discovery and your own platform for deeper work. A short course on Udemy introduces you to students. A longer program on your own platform is where you teach the ones who want more.

The second is a funnel builder for selling and a course platform for teaching. The funnel handles the launch and the checkout. The course platform handles everything after a student enrolls with you.

The principle is the same in both cases. Let each tool do the job it's built around.

## What Should You Do Next?

Before you open another feature table, try this. Write down one week of your course as a student would live it. What do they watch or read? What do they do? Who do they talk to, and when?

Then underline the sentence where the learning actually happens. It might be a lesson, a conversation, a live session or practice on their own. That sentence tells you which category to look at first. Only then go back to the feature tables, and use them to choose within that category.

If you're still torn, choose for the course you'll teach next, not the business you might run in five years. You'll learn more from teaching it than from another round of comparing.
